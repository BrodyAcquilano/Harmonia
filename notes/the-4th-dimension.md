## 1. The hidden phasor dimension

The hidden phasor note ended with the fifth dimension: the frequency domain itself, entered by the coordinate change. What can we say about that dimension? It is the relative phase change of force and velocity — or, the same thing said three ways, of energy and motion, or of time and position. All three pairs share one symmetry: converting one quantity into another based on mass. Force becomes velocity through the quarter-turn $i$; energy becomes motion; time becomes position. The mass is what sets the exchange rate.

The envelope of a system tells us how similar those two quantities are at any moment, in both time and space. Write the two reciprocal waves as usual,

$$
W_1(M_1,M_2) = A_1 e^{i(k_1 M_1 - \omega_1 M_2)}
$$

$$
W_2(M_2,M_1) = A_2 e^{i(k_2 M_2 - \omega_2 M_1)}
$$

and the envelope $E(\tau) = |W_1 + W_2|$ is the running measure of that similarity: how alike force and velocity are, how alike energy and motion are, at each point. Where the envelope is large, the two quantities agree; where it is small, they differ. But the envelope is never perfectly still, because there are always fluctuations from external sources.

## 2. A balanced push is a phase shift; acceleration is not

If there is an outside external force but it is balanced, then it just applies a phase shift without actually scaling the frequencies. The wave is multiplied by a pure rotation,

$$
W \to W \, e^{i\Delta\theta},
$$

and nothing else changes: the frequencies $\omega_1, \omega_2$ stay where they were. So we can just integrate again over the shifted dimension, either in space or in time — the shift is absorbed and the machinery carries on.

If there is acceleration in the system, we can't treat it as a simple phase shift. Acceleration scales the frequencies themselves; the similarity between the quantities changes unevenly. That is the dividing line for everything that follows: a phase shift preserves the symmetry, acceleration breaks it.

## 3. The convolution domain

Call the frequency-phase domain the domain of convolution: it is where we see how similar two quantities are. In the position–time domain, sums of balanced force and velocity just keep the overall energy proportional — the books balance. The similarity in the frequency domain is then the same similarity, but rotated, and the rotation angle is set by which direction the external force points.

If there is an imbalance in the external force, it appears as variable scaling of the similarities: one pair gets a different scaling than another. Then we have to add two branches in space, because we are integrating over a resultant vector that is not perpendicular to the eigenvector, and it doesn't have the same symmetry rule. The perpendicular case is the special one — the only one where the old symmetry still applies directly.

## 4. Merge branches, then rotate

The symmetry tells us that integrating over time can tell us about space: that is the $i$ rotation, the quarter-turn that converts one into the other. But if the resultant vector is not perpendicular to the eigenvector, we can't use that symmetry as-is — we have to use a rotated version of it.

So we merge branches, then we rotate. Summing those branches in the space domain becomes a product in the frequency domain, and a product is something we can integrate over. The order matters: merge first (collect the components in spacetime, where summation is honest), then rotate into the phase domain where the merged thing becomes one integrable product.

## 5. Rotation and projection in the phase domain

In this domain of convolution, where we see how similar two quantities are, we can only compare convolution products through rotation. To sum, we have to go back to the spacetime domain, because summation breaks the symmetry.

If the force is perpendicular, the rotation is such that all its values are rotated to be negative in the phase domain, so we can add them as the negative values. That is the clean case. But if there is another angle, we can do a second rotation: project or rotate the external force onto the eigenplane, or convert those values to see how they affect our eigen line and eigenplane — thus creating a new convolution, because we differentiated or integrated again. Every new rotation is a new convolution; every new convolution is another differentiation or integration of the relation.

## 6. In the time domain we add; in the phase domain we rotate

The two domains have different arithmetic. In the time domain we do it by adding resultant components: resultants sum, branches merge, the books balance in spacetime. In the phase domain we do it by rotating one convolution and projecting it onto a new line: sum all the changes along the rotation, then add the rotated version to the other convolution.

Both are the same operation seen from the two sides of the transform. Adding in time is rotating in phase; rotating in phase is adding in time. The symmetry is what lets us translate between them — as long as the perpendicular condition holds, or we have applied the corrective rotation.

## 7. The dimensionless collapse

But in the phase domain everything collapses to a single dimensionless quantity, so we can't have multiple branches there. There is just a single quantity that relates the relation of force and velocity to changes in position and time over a given 1D line. The phase domain is one-dimensional and unitless: it holds the relation, not the components.

However, there is never any way to break the total energy of the system into all of its individual components — and it gets harder as time passes, because entropy always increases. More entropy means more component branches, more phasors. This suggests something: that space is expanding in time. The number of eigenvectors relating space and time can only increase if the volume of space increases relative to some surface area. That is what relates space to time.

## 8. The convolution sphere

Imagine a convolution sphere that relates 3D space to its surface area. The surface area tells us how many eigenvectors there are; the sphere tells us how many states we have. We can draw points from anywhere on the surface and draw a line to the center — each line is an eigenvector, each surface point a state. And we can rotate in 3 axes of space and 2 in time.

The relation between the two is the relation between volume and surface: if the number of eigenvectors is going to grow, the volume of 3D space must grow relative to the 2D surface. New eigenvectors don't come from nowhere — they come from expansion.

## 9. Amplitude fluctuations on the sphere

Now allow the sphere to become a wave, and its amplitude bends around some center. There are deviations in the radius; the surface area of the sphere becomes non-uniform. It has peaks and valleys, but it has some baseline invariant state we can measure fluctuations from.

And the amplitude of the sphere's fluctuations for space and time are inverted: a high spot on one part of the sphere of velocity is a low spot for the sphere of force. That is how we are able to convolve the two quantities into a wave wrapped around space — the inversion is the convolution. Where velocity bulges out, force caves in, and the wave holds both at once.

## 10. Entropy, expansion, and counting new eigenstates

If entropy is always increasing at a constant rate, everywhere uniformly, then we get a sphere that is expanding. As it expands we get countably new points on the sphere. The number of eigenvectors we can draw from a point on the surface to the center increases based on the volume of 3D space to 2D time — volume to surface area.

If space expands at a constant rate everywhere, then we know how long this convolution sphere has been expanding: we can check the current energy fluctuations in a contained boundary and see how much they increase. From that we determine the rate of change of the number of eigenstates being added — which is basically adding new frequencies, which in the phase domain turns into energy fluctuations. Bodies changing direction means adding more eigenvectors. In the frequency domain this turns into a product of $T^2 / a^3$ — the Kepler ratio, the same $T^2 \propto a^3$ that the harmonies were built on, now counting states instead of orbits.

The rate of change of the increase in energy fluctuation, related to the change in the number of available eigenstates, becomes a scaling product that relates the surface of the sphere to some baseline. If we know the energy fluctuations and the rate they increase, we know the rate of change of expansion of the number of eigenstates. Thus we can predict how chaotic the universe is at a given time — based on statistics and probabilities, like particles in a gas or a fluid.

Or we can reverse the direction of the sphere's expansion: collapse the fluctuation and trace the eigenstates back to their origin. As the sphere collapses, time and space become more similar, and the number of eigenstates we need to describe the relationships reduces — which means the motion is more uniform and less turbulent, as if it was all coming from a single point. And that is the big bang theory.

## 11. Fourier decomposition of a local boundary

The energy fluctuation in a local boundary area can be broken up into component waves using the Fourier transform. We take the signal and decompose it. Because we know every wave has an exponential decay and an envelope of its decay, we can trace those wave amplitudes and frequencies back — and get the cosmic background radiation. The state of energy fluctuations at every point tells us every component of motion in the system for every resultant eigenvector.

However, there is an infinite number of eigenvectors; we don't know every body in space. But if we know the fluctuations in the energy within a boundary, we can break that wave into component frequencies, and in the frequency domain we can integrate. Then the three solvable things:

- if we know energy fluctuations and velocity changes, we can solve for mass;
- if we know the masses and the energy fluctuations, we can trace the bodies back in time and find their previous positions;
- if we know the mass and velocities, we can find the energy fluctuations at a given moment in time.

But we never have all the conditions required to explain everything.

## 12. One circle on the time sphere

We can measure the energy fluctuations and get the frequencies and trace them back in time — which is like knowing one circle on our time sphere. But we don't know all the masses, so the rest of the sphere becomes unknown. We can only trace back eigenvectors on that circle.

If we know all the masses in a system, they won't contain all the frequencies for all the eigenstates — so we get one geodesic of the sphere in the other time dimensions.

But what we can do is treat all the energy fluctuations in the system as resulting from one single mass that is described by all the eigenstates. That is essentially what the big bang does: it treats all the eigenstates as being from a single source, and traces back to it. Which is reasonable, because we know that fluctuations come from increased chaos — things changing direction, branches splitting into component directions — which causes more eigenstates. So if eigenstates are reduced, it means more uniform motion, less turbulence.

## 13. The resonator cube

You could imagine a giant resonator cube with every body in the universe vibrating in sync relative to each other at the same frequency as the origin, and all relative motion being the same — that would produce the same result. Linear motion is not the only type of motion that produces low entropy and uniform motion. Bodies all vibrating at the same frequency in spacetime, oscillating, produces the same result.

The vibration of each body could become more turbulent over time, and each node in the resonator cube could have its frequencies fluctuating and varying at different rates, or being phase-shifted — and that would also produce increasing entropy. But what would cause the frequencies to change from something stable to something new? It is very clear that could only happen when something is changing the underlying relation of mass at points in space and time — altering the symmetry.

## 14. Capacitance from a higher dimension

It's similar to using capacitance and inductors to alter the strength of an electric signal in computers. Space and time could be a giant array of particles in a higher dimension. The resonator cube starts with a single power source, a single vibrational frequency. A higher dimension introduces capacitance at every point in space and time, changing the underlying frequency of each relative component vibration in the resonator.

We sense this as distortion of time and space — or acceleration: the quantity that relates space and time at every point. The quantity that relates matter and time at each point is related to energy. This external vibration is added to each point like a capacitance. That extra signal itself could be composed of multiple frequencies and just be a resultant from several other dimensions leaking into our energy/time field. That is what dark energy is.

We could try to do a Fourier transform and find all the frequencies of this dark energy, and then we would know how many different frequencies are being introduced into the resonator, and from how many dimensions. Thus we would know not only the rate of how fast entropy changes, but also how many things are causing the change in entropy for the resultant.

However, since we can assume most radiation is within a finite range of the frequency spectrum, we can set boundary conditions and integrate over them to get the total resultant — and treat all the different frequencies of light as coming from a source that is angled in higher dimensions.

The rate of increase of entropy therefore indicates:
